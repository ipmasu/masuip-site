US Robotics and China Manufacturing: Strong Patents Do Not Equal a Strong Supply Chain

A short IP essay on robotics supply chains, patents, manufacturing capability, component control, and cross-border FTO.

By Ma Su Team2026-08-28Robotics Supply Chain

Chinese version

Recent reports on humanoid and industrial robot demand point to a practical reality: the United States is strong in software, AI models, and some core algorithms, but robot hardware still depends on global manufacturing capacity, including motors, reducers, sensors, batteries, structural parts, and assembly.

This shows that strong patents do not automatically mean a strong supply chain. Patents protect technical solutions, but mass-produced robots also require materials, machining, assembly, testing, and maintenance systems. Some component suppliers may not have famous brands, yet control tolerances, lifetime testing, heat treatment, control parameters, and process know-how, often as trade secrets rather than patents.

For robotics companies, FTO should not stop at whole-machine patents. A joint module may involve motor structure, reducer geometry, encoder mounting, thermal paths, cable routing, control algorithms, and assembly methods. Each supply-chain layer can hide patent or trade-secret risk.

The opportunity for Chinese companies is to turn manufacturing capability into patent portfolios. Around joint modules, dexterous hands, sensor fusion, low-cost manufacturing, maintenance diagnostics, and application scenarios, a patent map can move a company from supply-chain position to IP position.
